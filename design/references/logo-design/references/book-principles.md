## Airey: the business problem guides the form

### Brief before drawing

Identify the audience, need, offer, competition, desired associations, use cases,
and decision-makers. The brief becomes a common basis for evaluating proposals.
Turn broad adjectives into decisions that can be observed and discussed.

Evidence: PDF 55–62 / printed 42–49; PDF 72–74 / printed 59–61.

**Skill application:** Reuse known facts. Ask about material gaps. Connect each
proposed visual choice to a business fact and an intended audience impression.
Do not treat the owner's favorite style as proof of audience fit.

### Simplicity serves recognition and use

Simple structure is easier to recognize, remember, and reproduce across media.
Relevance means suitability for the business and audience; it does not require
a literal picture of the product. Durable identities depend on the underlying
idea more than on current styling. Distinction must remain when color is removed.

Evidence: PDF 35–44 / printed 22–31.

**Skill application:** Test what survives removing color and decorative details.
Simplification must retain a specific identifying feature. Minimalism by itself
does not make a logo distinctive.

### One dominant feature

A viewer often sees a logo briefly. Give the mark a clear feature to recognize.
Additional meanings can coexist when they form one coherent visual idea.
A collection of unrelated symbols asks too much of the viewer.

Evidence: PDF 45–52 / printed 32–39; PDF 194 / printed 181.

**Skill application:** Describe the intended memory feature in one sentence.
Inspect whether the drawing expresses that sentence without a long explanation.
The skill does not infer measured recall from a designer's opinion.

### Explore associations, then constructions

Word associations broaden possible directions. Loose sketching permits more
experiments before software precision makes a first idea expensive to abandon.
The quality of the idea matters more than drawing polish at this stage.

Evidence: PDF 103–110 / printed 90–97; PDF 115–122 / printed 102–109.

**Skill application:** Explore more ideas than the final requested count. Vary
meaning and construction. Use available tools for fast visual exploration;
do not pretend generated studies are hand-drawn sketches.

### Compare form before color

In the Woodmere example, distinct concepts were first presented in black and
white. Color followed the selection of a structural direction. This kept palette
preferences from obscuring the idea. Context mockups then helped the client judge
how the identity would operate.

Evidence: PDF 123–129 / printed 110–116.

**Skill application:** Compare candidates with equal visual prominence and neutral
presentation. Introduce consistent real-use examples after the plain marks.
Do not let a dramatic rendering substitute for a clear design.

### Redesign and feedback require reasons

Existing elements may carry recognition. Preserve or replace them according to
what they contribute. Present the rationale with each option, gather reactions
about the business objective, and use a fair visual comparison to resolve a
disagreement. Client feedback may improve a preferred concept.

Evidence: PDF 75–88 / printed 62–75; PDF 131–153 / printed 118–140.

**Skill application:** Compare refinement and replacement. Avoid a mashup of
unrelated concepts. Do not use a fabricated policy to pressure the client.

### Selection and delivery

Airey generally presents a small set of strong concepts and warns against weak
additions. He also emphasizes readable lettering, coherent type, one-color and
reverse versions, several sizes, physical materials, and consistent application.
The logo is part of the brand, not a replacement for the business's conduct.

Evidence: PDF 182–189 / printed 169–176; PDF 193–204 / printed 180–191.

**Skill application:** Honor the user's requested count through sufficient
exploration. Deliver only that stage's actual assets and measured constraints.
Use vector masters for production; label raster explorations accurately.

## Modernism: a vocabulary for making and comparing forms

### Explicit ideas in the available text

**A systematic identity extends beyond the mark.** The introductory essay
describes how coordinated color, typography, imagery, and manuals became part of
identity programs. It also discusses reproduction tradeoffs of later digital
effects. It does not justify a blanket ban on color or dimensional work.

Evidence: PDF 6 / printed 12–13.

**Geometry offers a visual vocabulary.** The geometric introduction describes
circles, triangles, rectangles, and squares as material for clear signs. A design
may also refer to the name or business in a meaning that emerges on another look.

Evidence: PDF 122, photographed geometric introduction; the printed number is
not visible in this crop. PDF 2 lists the Geometric section beginning at 56.

**Operations extend basic shapes.** The Effect introduction names techniques such
as reflection, duplication, and cropping, and observes that several operations
can contribute to one finished identity.

Evidence: PDF 54, photographed Effect introduction; printed number not visible.
PDF 2 lists the Effect section beginning at 154 and its subcategories.

**An identity must carry through applications.** The Daiei case connects a simple
symbol to packaging, uniforms, treatments, and a manual. Its type supports the
symbol rather than competing with it.

Evidence: PDF 47 / printed 102–103.

### Visual deductions from the examples

These are observations for practice, not claims that the book states a universal
design rule or proves commercial effectiveness.

- **A few shapes can yield very different silhouettes.** The Basic Forms spread
  varies proportion, arrangement, and negative space using a small geometric
  vocabulary. Change the relationship among elements, not only their color.
  Evidence: PDF 36 / printed 72–73.
- **Combining letters is a construction problem.** The Two Letters spread varies
  shared strokes, enclosure, overlap, and figure-ground relationships. A monogram
  must still be checked for letter order and unintended readings.
  Evidence: PDF 88 / printed 322–323.
- **A useful system can repeat a design decision.** The Daiei applications show
  how a characteristic mark and controlled treatments can connect different
  surfaces without redrawing a new identity for each one.
  Evidence: PDF 47 / printed 102–103.

**Skill application:** Use these operations to generate alternatives, then judge
each against the brief. Modernist form is a resource, not a required style.
A serif wordmark, figurative symbol, or more expressive treatment may fit better.
Do not trace an archived logo or claim originality because its colors changed.

## Where the books complement each other

Airey provides the client and design process: establish the problem, explore,
select, explain, refine, and apply. Modernism provides a broad set of formal
constructions and examples of identity systems. Use the process to decide which
formal experiments are relevant.

The two sources need not be forced into a false contradiction. Airey's advice to
give a mark one dominant feature concerns the viewer's attention. Multiple formal
operations can produce that single coherent feature.

## Present-day additions in this skill

The following are implementation choices derived from those principles. They are
not quotations or historical prescriptions from either book:

- Specific CSS-pixel inspection sizes, browser previews, and profile crop checks.
- An explicit five-direction exploration when the user requests five.
- Pass/revise/not-checked records instead of an invented quantitative quality score.
- Separating AI-generated raster concepts from inspected vector masters.
- Recording artwork and font provenance and the scope of a similarity check.
- Separating visible website branding from favicon, sharing, and metadata changes.

Neither the books nor these notes establish legal clearance, conversion lift,
search ranking benefits, or objectively measured customer trust for a new design.
