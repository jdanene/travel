# Logo design

Create an identifying mark that suits the business, survives its intended uses,
and gives people a clear feature to recognize. A polished rendering alone does
not establish any of these qualities.

This tracked folder contains original written guidance and agent metadata. The
supplied books and reproduced artwork remain local and are not included in Git.
The written workflow works without those files.

- Read [book-principles.md](references/book-principles.md) when beginning a
  design or explaining the reasoning. It separates the authors' ideas from
  visual deductions and present-day production checks.
- For concept development, use the study notes in
  [visual-examples.md](references/visual-examples.md). Compare at least two
  different construction methods before choosing one. If the local books are
  available, inspect the relevant pages before making new visual claims.
- Use [source-guide.md](references/source-guide.md) to locate optional original
  sources when a decision needs deeper evidence. Read the relevant page and its
  context instead of inferring a rule from an isolated logo.

The supplied Modernism PDF is an incomplete selection of photographed spreads.
The guide records that limit. Keep the books and reference artwork out of Git
and public client exports. Follow the project's inspection rules; these guides
do not override restrictions on screenshots or repeated rendering.

## 1. Establish what the mark must do

Use facts and preferences already supplied. Ask only for an omission that
would change the design. Write a short working brief:

- Exact public name and required descriptor. Separate legal wording from logo text.
- Audience and the situation in which they encounter the business.
- The business quality the identity should communicate, with supporting evidence.
- Primary uses and their constraints: website header, profile icon, sign, vehicle,
  packaging, clothing, or another actual application.
- Existing recognition worth retaining, and the reason for changing the logo.
- Relevant competing identities and recurring visual conventions.
- Client preferences, required elements, exclusions, and the requested deliverable.

Distinguish a preference from a requirement. For example, a client liking a serif
monogram does not establish that a serif is readable on their small sign.
Resolve that question through the design. Do not invent business history,
credentials, taglines, or a market position to justify a symbol.

For a redesign, compare a measured evolution with a more substantial change.
Identify what each preserves and improves. Do not discard recognition solely
because a different style is fashionable.

## 2. Explore ideas before polishing shapes

Map associations from the audience, name, product or service, and distinguishing
quality. Follow some associations beyond the literal object. A plumbing company
may suggest reliability or comfort as well as a pipe; the mark need not depict
every service.

Sketch several constructions before selecting finalists. Use loose drawings,
simple vectors, or generated concept studies suited to the available tools.
The purpose is to vary the idea quickly. Do not spend the exploration phase
perfecting shadows, color, or mockups.

For each promising direction, record:

1. The idea in one concrete sentence.
2. The visible feature that expresses it.
3. Its relationship to the brief.
4. Its main risk, such as ambiguous lettering or a common category symbol.

Explore suitable families: wordmark, single letter, monogram, geometric symbol,
or a simplified figurative mark. These are ways to explore, not mandatory quotas.
An independent symbol is optional.

If the user requests five alternatives, develop five strong alternatives. Each
must differ in its central idea or construction. Five colors, fonts, or frames
around the same mark do not count. Reject filler and develop another direction.

## 3. Refine in one color

Make the identifying structure work as a solid dark mark on a plain light field
before using color to distinguish it. Explain exceptions when color is essential
to the brief, and still determine how the identity reproduces without it.

Use these principles to make decisions:

| Principle | Design action | Failure to inspect |
|---|---|---|
| Simplicity | Remove a detail and compare what recognition loses. | An empty generic shape is simple but not distinctive. |
| Relevance | Connect the tone and construction to the audience and business. | A literal service icon can still feel wrong for the audience. |
| Longevity | Base the idea on a durable business quality. | An effect or current style carries the entire identity. |
| Distinction | Give the silhouette or lettering a specific identifying feature. | It becomes interchangeable with the comparison set. |
| Recognition | Keep one dominant feature; let other details support it. | Several equally loud tricks compete for attention. |
| Reduction | Inspect the real small application before adding fine detail. | Gaps close, strokes vanish, or letters change identity. |
| Coherence | Make symbol, spacing, and lettering belong together. | A stock-looking icon sits beside unrelated type. |

Work on optical balance, not just coordinate equality. Compare the visual weight
of straight and curved strokes; inspect counters, joins, diagonals, overshoots,
edge spacing, and the space between symbol and name. Use a grid when it helps
construction. Do not invent a geometric formula to explain a finished shape.

Treat negative space as part of the drawing. A secondary reading should reward
attention without making the primary name or silhouette difficult to read.
Inspect monograms for the intended letter order and accidental readings.

Choose type for the business and the mark. Adjust kerning by letter pair. Check
the public name at its actual display size. A descriptor is subordinate but must
remain readable wherever it is included; omit it in a declared small-use variant
when necessary. Do not shrink the whole lockup until its words disappear.

Use the historical examples to study operations such as repetition, overlap,
rotation, enclosure, contrast, and figure-ground relationships. Develop a new
construction for the brief. An existing mark is evidence to study, not an asset
to trace or a template to rename.

## 4. Check the idea in use

Read [review-and-delivery.md](references/review-and-delivery.md) before selecting
or handing off work. Inspect the files that will actually be delivered.

Compare alternatives at equal perceived size, on the same neutral background,
with the same level of finish. Show the plain mark before attractive mockups.
Then use the same relevant applications for each finalist. For website work,
inspect the current desktop and mobile header, not only a large presentation.

Perform the checks that match the brief: small size, dark background, one-color
printing, letter readability, symbol/name balance, and distinctive appearance
beside relevant identities. Record pass, revise, or not checked with evidence.
Do not convert an untested impression into a measured claim about memorability,
customer trust, conversion, or uniqueness.

If the user supplied no color direction, introduce a restrained palette after
the structure works. Show that color supports the same identifying feature.
Do not give a weaker shape a more impressive presentation to balance the set.

## 5. Explain and refine

For each finalist, show the design, its idea, why it fits, and its main tradeoff.
Use plain descriptions of visible features. Avoid unsupported stories about
symbols that viewers cannot reasonably see.

Recommend the strongest option against the brief. Invite feedback about the
business impression and the use case. When the client asks for a change, try it
and compare the consequence. Preserve a coherent concept instead of combining
every preferred detail into one mark.

Use the user's requested number and format. Airey's usual small presentation set
is a way to avoid weak choices, not a reason to refuse a requested five-way study.

## 6. Deliver the right stage

Label concept studies, refined candidates, and production assets accurately.
If the task excludes imagery, label the result a written proposal, not a logo.
An image-generation tool can help explore and present an idea; its raster result
is not a vector master. Reconstruct and inspect clean vectors when production
deliverables are requested. Do not describe an automatic trace as hand-refined.

Keep client-specific briefs, concepts, and review notes with that project. Keep
this skill reusable. If integrating a preview into an existing website, change
only the requested identity surfaces; treat favicons, social previews, metadata,
and production publication as separate surfaces whose scope comes from the user.

Before handing off, state what was made, where it is, what was inspected, and any
remaining issue that affects the next decision. Do not claim the books were read
in full when the supplied files or actual review covered only part of them.
